Self-Leveling Concrete Problems & Solutions

Editorial TeamLast updated September 2026
Quick answer

Most self-leveling concrete problems trace back to one of a few causes: an incorrect water ratio, missing or wrong primer, pouring outside the product's rated depth, or environmental issues like drafts and temperature swings during cure. Identifying which of these applies usually points straight to the fix.

Surface Isn't Level

A pour that dries with visible high or low spots is usually caused by mixing inconsistency, working the material after it started to set, or pouring on a substrate that wasn't structurally flat to begin with. See the full breakdown in Why Is My Self-Leveling Concrete Not Level?

Cracking

Cracking is most often linked to pouring too thick in one lift, an incorrect water ratio, or movement in the substrate below. The detailed causes and repair options are covered in Self-Leveling Concrete Cracking: Causes & Fixes.

Bubbles and Pinholes

Trapped air that isn't released with a spiked roller before the compound sets can leave small bubbles or pinholes in the cured surface. Excess moisture rising from an unsealed substrate can cause similar pinholing. Rolling the wet surface thoroughly and priming as directed are the two most effective preventive steps.

Peeling or Delamination

Peeling or delamination — where the cured layer separates from the substrate — is almost always a bond failure, usually from skipped or incorrect primer, dust or grease left on the substrate before pouring, or moisture in the substrate exceeding the product's limit. The affected area typically needs to be removed, the substrate properly prepped and primed, and the section re-poured.

Uneven Texture or Color

Blotchy color or an inconsistent texture across a pour is often a sign of uneven mixing between batches, inconsistent water ratio from batch to batch, or pouring in patches under different temperature and humidity conditions (for example, working across a doorway with a draft on one side).

When to Fix vs. Redo

Minor high spots can sometimes be ground down; small hairline cracks in a decorative or to-be-covered surface are sometimes acceptable under flooring, depending on the flooring manufacturer's tolerance. Bond failure, significant cracking, or areas that stay soft or crumbly generally need to be cut out and re-poured rather than patched over.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I pour a new layer over a failed self-leveling pour?

Only if the existing layer is fully cured, sound, and clean — pouring over a delaminating or crumbling layer will usually fail again. Removing the affected section is generally more reliable.

How long should I wait before deciding a pour has failed?

Most self-leveling compounds reach their intended hardness within the manufacturer's stated cure window (often 24–72 hours); ongoing softness or dusting after that point is a sign of a problem, not just slow cure.
