Self-Leveling Concrete: Complete Guide

Editorial TeamLast updated September 2026
Quick answer

Self-leveling concrete is a cement-based, polymer-modified compound that flows out to a flat, level surface under its own weight. It's used to correct uneven subfloors before installing tile, vinyl, hardwood, or carpet, and typically cures walkable in 24 hours and ready for flooring in 2–3 days depending on thickness and product.

What Is Self-Leveling Concrete?

Self-leveling concrete (also called a self-leveling underlayment or self-leveling compound) is a cementitious, polymer-modified powder that's mixed with water into a pourable slurry. Once poured, it spreads and settles into a flat plane using gravity and its own fluid consistency, correcting dips, high spots, and minor unevenness in a subfloor.

It is not the same material used to pour a driveway or a structural slab. Standard concrete is stiff, needs to be screeded and floated by hand, and is designed for structural load-bearing. Self-leveling compounds are formulated to flow, so they need far less manual finishing.

How It Works

The mix contains fine aggregate, Portland cement or a blended binder, and polymer additives that reduce viscosity so the material flows like a thick pancake batter. Once poured, gravity does most of the leveling work; installers use a gauge rake to spread it into rough position and a spiked roller to release trapped air bubbles.

When to Use It

Common situations where self-leveling concrete is the right material
SituationWhy it fits
Uneven concrete slab before tile or vinylCorrects dips and high spots the flooring manufacturer requires to be within tolerance
Radiant floor heat installationsEncapsulates heating elements in a flat, thermally conductive layer
Transitioning between rooms of different floor heightsBuilds up a low area to match an adjoining surface
Repairing a patchy or previously-repaired slabProvides one continuous, flat working surface

It is generally not the right choice for structural repairs, filling large voids under a slab, or exterior grading — see our underlayment guide and thickness guide for the depth limits that usually decide this.

Underlayment vs. Structural Concrete

"Self-leveling concrete" and "self-leveling underlayment" are often used interchangeably in the trade, though underlayment products are usually formulated for thinner pours and interior flooring prep, while some self-leveling concretes are rated for slightly deeper structural-adjacent repairs. Always check the manufacturer's technical data sheet for the rated depth range, since this varies by product line, not just by brand.

The Basic Process

  1. Confirm subfloor is clean, structurally sound, and within the product's moisture limits.
  2. Prime the surface (see our primer guide) and let it dry fully.
  3. Mix the compound to the manufacturer's water ratio using a low-speed drill and paddle.
  4. Pour and spread with a gauge rake, working from the deepest area first.
  5. Pass a spiked roller through the wet material to release air.
  6. Protect the area from drafts, direct sun, and foot traffic while it cures.

The full step-by-step version, including mixing ratios and common mistakes, is in How to Use Self-Leveling Concrete.

Cure and Dry Times

Typical (product-dependent) timelines — always confirm against your product's data sheet
StageTypical timeframe
Light foot traffic3–6 hours
Walkable / next coat24 hours
Ready for flooring (thin pour)24–48 hours
Ready for flooring (deeper pour)3–7+ days

Humidity, temperature, and pour depth all extend these times. Manufacturer data sheets are the authority here, not general guidance like this page.

Choosing a Product

Products from manufacturers such as MAPEI, Sika, Rapid Set, Henry, Quikrete, Ardex, Laticrete, and Akona differ in rated depth, working time, and required primer. This site does not rank specific products or invent specifications for them — always confirm coverage, depth range, and cure time against the current technical data sheet for the exact product you're buying, since formulas change between revisions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is self-leveling concrete the same as floor leveling compound?

Yes, in most trade usage these terms describe the same category of pourable, self-flowing cementitious product used to flatten subfloors.

Can I use self-leveling concrete as a finished floor?

Some polished or dyed self-leveling products are designed to be a finished floor, but standard gray self-leveling compound is intended to be covered by tile, vinyl, hardwood, or carpet.

Do I always need primer first?

Most manufacturers require or strongly recommend primer on porous or previously sealed substrates to control absorption and improve bonding. Check your specific product's instructions.
